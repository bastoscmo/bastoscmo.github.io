One of the fundamental challenges in quantum mechanics is describing systems with many interacting electrons. While the Schrödinger equation provides an exact description, solving it analytically for systems with more than one electron is impossible without approximations. This is where the **Hartree-Fock (HF) method** becomes an essential tool for physicists and chemists.

In my latest study note, I revisit the theoretical foundations of this method, tracing its origins back to the early 20th century.

## From Thomas-Fermi to Hartree-Fock

The journey begins with the statistical considerations of **Thomas and Fermi (1927)**, who attempted to approximate electron distribution using electron density. While useful for qualitative descriptions, this model struggled with properties dependent on valence electrons, such as chemical bonding.

**Douglas Hartree** later proposed using the variational method with a "test" function composed of a product of individual electron wavefunctions. However, this approach failed to account for a crucial quantum property: the antisymmetric character of fermions.

The **Hartree-Fock method** refines this by employing the **Slater determinant**. This ensures the wavefunction satisfies the Pauli exclusion principle, introducing the concept of exchange energy—a purely quantum mechanical effect arising from the indistinguishability of particles.

## Deep Dive into the Derivation

I have prepared a detailed document that walks through the mathematical derivation of the Hartree-Fock method. It covers:
